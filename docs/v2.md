# What a second version could be

Three questions were put to three researchers in parallel: what comparable
tools do that this one does not, what the visual language should become, and
what the code would have to change to carry it. This is the synthesis, kept
short enough to argue with.

Nothing here is committed to. It is a menu, ordered.

---

## 1. The one gap everybody found

**There is no clock.** Every survey of desktop gadgets, sidebars and menu-bar
tools puts clock and date first, the nearest competitor puts one at the top of
its sidebar by default, and the loudest complaint about losing the Windows 7
gadgets was about seeing the date without clicking anything. A bar that is
permanently on screen and cannot tell you the time is odd.

After that, in order of how often it came up: **battery**, **a trend rather
than an instant**, **an alert that interrupts rather than a colour that waits
to be noticed**, and **an answer to "why"** — a reading at ninety per cent
raises a question the bar cannot answer.

---

## 2. Features, in the order they are worth doing

| | Feature | Why | Weight |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Clock and date**, with a calendar when clicked | The most-used gadget there is, and absent | S |
| 2 | **Threshold alerts with an action** — warn at X for N seconds, then a toast, a sound, or a command | Colour only works if you happen to be looking | M |
| 3 | **A polling budget** — one poller off the interface thread, per-source intervals, backing off while a game is in front | Three bars, forever, on somebody's machine. Graphics-driver queries are a documented cause of stutter in other tools | M |
| 4 | **History** — a sparkline on the bar, minute / ten minutes / hour when clicked | A number with no past cannot say whether things are getting worse | M |
| 5 | **The look follows the desktop** — solid over a maximised window, out of the way of a full-screen game, dimmed when idle | The backdrop settings exist; making them conditional is most of the work already done | S–M |
| 6 | **Hotkeys** — show, hide, focus the bar, reach slot N | The nearest competitor ships this; there is no keyboard path in or out today | M |
| 7 | **Slots that appear only when they matter** — above a threshold, on battery, while a process runs | Costly on a menu bar, cheap here: a slot is a numbered place, and an empty one is simply empty | M |
| 8 | **"Which programs are doing this"** when a reading is clicked | Nothing on a Windows edge bar does it. Processor and memory are honest; per-process graphics and network are not, and should be left out rather than guessed | M–L |
| 9 | **Sound: output device and microphone mute** | The most-listed quick toggle anywhere. Master Audio Switcher already solved the hard half | M |
| 10 | **Battery**, hidden on machines without one | The media widget's hide-when-idle pattern is the model | S |
| 11 | **A command line** — extend `--exit` to toggling a dock and switching a profile | Scripting and Stream Deck integration for the price of an argument parser | S |
| 12 | **Bar profiles** — "Work", "Gaming", switched by hand | A bar that suits a game does not suit a meeting. Switching *automatically* on the foreground app is the interesting version and should wait: a bar that rearranges itself as you alt-tab is either brilliant or unusable, and that is not decidable from a specification | M |
| 13 | **Running programs show they are running**, and clicking focuses rather than launching again | Turns a strip of launchers into a strip of tasks. The indicator must be absent rather than wrong — store apps and browser shortcuts cannot be matched reliably | M |
| 14 | **A widget that runs a command** and shows one line of its output | One widget buys every niche reading there could be. It is also a way to run arbitrary code, which is fine on one person's machine and disqualifying for a shop | M |

Held back: **weather** is the only thing here that would make this a network
client, and it brings an API key, a privacy story and an offline state with it.

## 3. Three things not to build

**A plugin system for other people's widgets.** It is the obvious next step,
which is why it needs saying out loud. The reason this program's widgets can
draw graphs and colour their own warnings is that each one owns its view model
and its template; a public interface re-imposes exactly the ceiling the
upstream dock has. And the blast radius is unusual here — this program reserves
part of the desktop through the shell, so a crash in somebody else's plugin
costs the user their work area, not just a widget. The command-output widget
gets most of the benefit and none of the interface.

**A launcher or a search box on the bar.** Half a dozen good ones exist, they
are all keyboard-first and gone in two seconds, and that is the opposite of
what a permanently visible bar is for. This sits *beside* the command palette
on purpose.

**An in-game overlay.** It looks adjacent to the sensor work and is not: it
means hooking the graphics presentation path, anti-cheat exposure, and a
measured cost that can halve a frame rate. The right behaviour toward a
full-screen game is items 3 and 5 — notice it, poll less, get out of the way.

## 4. How it should look

The reference program's depth model has one rule, and it is the opposite of
what decoration would suggest:

> **Containers go down. Controls come up.**

A card is *darker* than the body — a recess milled into the panel. A control
sitting in that recess is *lighter* than the body — it rises back out. Nothing
is ever a lighter box on a darker box for its own sake; lightness always says
which way.

A recess is a darker fill, a hairline border, and a short dark gradient down
from its top edge. A raised thing is a gentle top-to-bottom gradient, a
one-pixel lit line along its top edge, and a soft shadow beneath. Pressed
inverts all three. The light is upper-left, one source, always. That is now
implemented, and it is what the bar should inherit next.

**At bar scale**, thirty to forty-eight points thick, what survives is the
palette, the recessed rail, the small caps with wide tracking, and the press
behaviour. What does not survive is anything with a blur over about six points
— shadows are clipped at the window edge — and anything needing two lines.

The proposal worth taking from the design work: **the bar is the front panel
and the hover chip is the manual.** The chip is a popup, so it can escape the
bar's few pixels and carry the full language — a proper card, a real shadow, a
reading with its explanation underneath. Everything that will not fit on a
forty-point strip goes there rather than being squeezed onto it.

Widget ideas in that idiom, in rough order of appeal: an **LCD readout** on
grey-green glass where unlit segments stay faintly visible (which also solves,
by material rather than by font, the problem that digits of different widths
make a reading jitter); a **segmented level meter** with a peak that holds and
falls; a **two-lane network meter** with its scale printed on the panel below
the glass rather than inside it; and the **knurled edge of a knob**, which is
all of a knob that fits.

One note on colour worth keeping: the reference's orange and a blue of the
same lightness are interchangeable without re-deriving a single contrast
decision. If a blue of this program's own is ever wanted instead of the one
Windows is set to, that is how to choose it.

## 5. What the code would need first

**Before anything else: the tests cannot reach the application.** The test
project references the core, the sensors and the interop layer, and not the
app. Every piece of arithmetic worth testing — how wide a slot is, what a
rebuild is allowed to cost, when a warning colour is allowed to fall back —
lives in the app and is therefore checked only by looking at the screen. The
slot arithmetic was moved out for exactly this reason; the rest should follow.

**The extensibility answer that is worth the work** is not a plugin interface.
It is one widget type whose settings name a sensor, a label, a unit, an icon
and two thresholds — because all five of those are already parameters of the
existing reading chip. That turns "I want the sensor you did not list" from a
rebuild into a form, and adds no public interface at all.

**One structural change worth making regardless:** a widget's template is
looked up by name from the application's resources, and a missing one is a
warning in the log and a widget that silently does not appear. It should be a
failure at startup instead.
